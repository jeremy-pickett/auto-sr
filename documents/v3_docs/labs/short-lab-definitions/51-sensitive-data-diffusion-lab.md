## The phenomenon

A file containing personal data is created in one place. Someone shares a link. Someone else downloads it and emails it. It is attached to a ticket, exported to a spreadsheet for a report, copied into a presentation, backed up nightly, replicated to a disaster recovery site, indexed by a search tool, and synced to three laptops.

Two years later the organization is asked where that data lives and cannot answer. Under data protection regimes this is not merely embarrassing — the obligation to delete on request presupposes knowing where the copies are.

The mechanism is entirely mundane. Nobody is attacking anything. Each copy is made by someone doing their job, and the aggregate is a distribution nobody chose and nobody tracks.

## What the domain already knows

**The commercial category is mature.** Data loss prevention, data security posture management, and cloud data discovery tools scan storage, classify content, and map where sensitive data resides. Classification by pattern matching, fingerprinting, and increasingly by model-based labelling is standard. The market is large and the products work reasonably.

**Data lineage is a solved problem where systems are instrumented.** In data warehouses and pipeline platforms, lineage tracking records which datasets derive from which — automatically, and completely, within the instrumented boundary.

**The unmeasured region is where it leaves the instrumentation**: a screenshot, a manual copy into a personal document, an export to a laptop, a message to a colleague. This is well understood as the hard part, and it is hard for reasons that have nothing to do with mechanism complexity.

**Branching process theory covers the abstract structure.** Copying is reproduction: each copy may or may not produce further copies. The mathematics of branching processes is classical and complete — extinction probability, expected population size, and the critical threshold at reproduction number one all have closed forms dating to the nineteenth century.

## Where the shortcut holds, and where it breaks

**Reducible — and this is the Lab's central problem, more so than anywhere else in Family H.**

If each copy independently produces some number of further copies with a given distribution, the total population, its growth or extinction, and the threshold between them are given by branching process theory in closed form. Expected number of copies after *n* generations is the reproduction number to the *n*. Extinction probability is the fixed point of the generating function. There is nothing to simulate.

Even the network version is largely covered: spread of a copyable item over a contact graph is epidemic modelling (#57), with the same analytic thresholds.

**Irreducible — a genuinely short list:**

- **Structural heterogeneity in copying rates.** Some locations are copy amplifiers — a shared drive that gets backed up and indexed, a reporting pipeline that fans out — and where data lands early determines how much it multiplies. That is arrangement-dependent, though a weighted branching process handles much of it analytically too.
- **Deletion racing propagation.** A deletion request must reach copies faster than copying creates new ones. This is a genuine race with a threshold, and non-monotonicity (removal) is what takes it outside the clean branching results.
- **Re-derivation after deletion.** A copy deleted from a warehouse is regenerated by the pipeline that produced it. That feedback loop — deletion and recreation from an upstream source — is the mechanism behind the practical impossibility of complete deletion, and it is not a branching process.

**The lens, stated plainly.** This Lab illustrates a failure mode worth naming precisely because it is easy to miss: **a phenomenon can be spatially extended, multi-step, and hard in practice while being mathematically reducible.** Data sprawl is genuinely difficult, but its difficulty is *instrumentation and discovery*, not dynamics. The organization cannot find the copies. It is not that the copies' distribution is unpredictable — it is that nobody is looking.

An SCR Lab would model a process that is well described by classical mathematics, and would contribute nothing to the actual problem, which is a visibility problem.

## What a Cell would carry

A location or system: whether a copy of the tracked data is present, classification label, access breadth, retention policy, and copy count. Bounded scalars; §13.1 met easily — the state is not the difficulty.

**Layout is a Network World** — Connections are the paths along which copying happens: share links, exports, backups, sync relationships, pipeline edges.

The one genuinely interesting structural note: **copies at one location can create locations that did not exist** (a new export destination, a new laptop), so the World's population grows. That is the changing-participant-count problem again, shared with #18, #20, and #50.

## Honest strength assessment

*My assessment, not a fit review (§30). It carries no standing and does not promote this entry.*

**Weak. I would grade it so, and I would place it at the bottom of Family H alongside #53.**

The catalog says "the mechanism is mundane and local; the outcome is the part organizations get wrong." That is accurate and it is precisely the argument against. The outcome is not got wrong because it is unpredictable; it is got wrong because nobody instruments the copying. The gap is observational, not computational, and SCR does not close observational gaps.

Every one of the position paper's criteria points down:

- The dynamics are covered by classical branching process theory.
- There is no live scientific controversy.
- The incumbents are commercial discovery tools solving the actual (visibility) problem.
- The interesting difficulty is data collection, which a simulation does not help with.

**What is nonetheless worth taking from it — and it is a catalog-level lesson rather than a Lab.** This entry is the clearest example of a **rejection reason distinct from the others in this catalog**: not "the mechanism is global" (#42, #43), not "the agents use global information" (#60), not "the substrate moves" (#49), but **"the mathematics is already closed-form and the real problem is measurement."**

A catalog that can articulate four distinct rejection reasons is a considerably more useful instrument than one that can only say "poor fit," and SCR-F §30 explicitly treats a failed Lab as evidence about the platform's boundary. This one should be written up as such.

**The upside worth being excited about.** Honestly, little, and the honest answer is more valuable than a strained one. The narrow residue — **deletion racing re-derivation**, where a delete is undone by an upstream pipeline regenerating the record — is a genuine non-monotone feedback loop with real regulatory consequence, and it is not covered by branching process theory. If any part of this deserves attention it is that, and it belongs in a Lab about pipeline lineage rather than about diffusion.

**The challenges, in order of severity.**

1. **The dynamics are analytically solved** by classical theory.
2. **The real problem is discovery**, which simulation does not address.
3. **No reference data** — organizations that could measure copy distributions do not publish them, for obvious reasons.
4. **Mature commercial incumbents** solving the actual problem.
5. **Privacy and regulatory sensitivity**, which raises the cost of any over-claim (§30.7).

## Non-claims

This Lab does not assess data handling in any real organization, does not support compliance or regulatory claims, and produces nothing suitable for privacy, legal, or security decisions. It is ungraded and may fail its fit review (§30, §41, §43).
